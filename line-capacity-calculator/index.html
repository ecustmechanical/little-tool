How it works

Why line capacity is a diameter problem, not a strength problem

Picture the spool as a fixed cylinder of empty space. Every yard of line you wind on consumes a slice of that space, and the size of the slice is set by the line's cross-sectional area — which grows with the square of its diameter. Double a line's diameter and one yard of it takes four times the room. Halve it and you fit four times the yardage.

This is why reel manufacturers rate spools in monofilament. Mono has been the reference standard for decades, so nearly every reel prints a number like "10 lb / 200 yd" on the spool face. That rating describes a specific mono diameter, and from it you can convert to anything else with the ratio of the diameters:

L₂ = L₁ × (d₁² / d₂²)

where L₁ and d₁ are the rated mono length and diameter, and d₂ is your target line's diameter. The calculator above does exactly this — you pick the mono rating, type in the yardage, choose your braid, fluorocarbon or mono, and get back the spool that fits.

Using the result

If the number comes out far below the spool you wanted (say, only 120 yd of 30 lb braid on a 2500 reel), you have two options: buy a smaller spool of line, or step up the reel size. If it comes out at 300+ yd, you're set — many anglers choose a 150 yd braid spool to leave room for a mono backing that grips the arbor.

One caveat before you order: braid diameter varies meaningfully by brand and construction. An 8-strand premium braid at 30 lb is often thinner than a budget 4-strand labeled 30 lb. Where precision matters — capacity-critical offshore or surf setups — read the diameter in millimeters off the spool label rather than trusting the lb rating alone. This calculator uses typical averages as a planning baseline, and the result is best treated as "which spool to buy," not "to the last yard."

Fill it right

Whatever line you choose, stop at about 90–95% of the spool's lip. Underfilling adds friction as the line peels off a smaller diameter spool, which measurably shortens your cast. Overfilling lets loose coils spring off the lip mid-cast and wind-knot your braid. Fill to within roughly 1/8 inch (3 mm) of the lip and you'll never think about it again.

Line capacity — frequently asked questions

How much braid fits on a spinning reel rated for mono?

Often 1.5–2.5× the mono rating, because braid is much thinner at the same pound test. For example, a reel rated 200 yd of 12 lb mono typically holds 300+ yd of 30 lb braid. The calculator converts using the diameter² ratio of the two lines.

Why can't I just compare pound test?

Pound test measures breaking strength, not physical size. A 30 lb braid can be the same diameter as 8 lb mono, so it occupies far less spool volume per yard. Spool capacity is determined by line volume, which scales with diameter squared — not lb rating.

How full should I fill my reel spool?

Within about 1/8 inch (3 mm) of the spool lip — roughly 90–95% fill. Underfilling costs casting distance because line peels off a smaller spool diameter; overfilling makes loose line spring off the lip in loops and tangle.

What is the mono rating on my reel spool?

Nearly every spinning, baitcasting and conventional reel lists a monofilament capacity printed on the spool, for example "10 lb / 200 yd". That rating is the reference point this calculator converts into braid or fluorocarbon capacity.

Do braid capacity numbers vary by brand?

Yes. Actual braid diameter varies by brand and construction (4-strand vs 8-strand), and budget braids often run thicker than labeled. Treat results as planning estimates and always check the spool label diameter in mm when precision matters.

Why does diameter matter more than material?

A reel spool is a fixed volume. A yard of line consumes an amount of that volume proportional to its cross-sectional area, which scales with the square of the diameter. Two lines of the same diameter — even different materials — fill a spool almost identically.
