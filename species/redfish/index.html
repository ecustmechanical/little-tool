Redfish Rig: The Complete Setup Guide

Scientific: Sciaenops ocellatus Water: Saltwater (inshore) Typical: 18–30 in · 3–10 lb Trophy: 27 in+ slot · bulls to 40 lb

Redfish — the red drum of the Gulf and Atlantic coasts — are the inshore angler's bread-and-butter: hard-fighting, sight-fishable, and willing to eat both bait and artificials. They spend their lives in some of the most punishing water a rod can touch: shallow flats where they tail in inches of water, oyster bars that can shred a line in one pass, and grass beds that grab a hooked fish before you can turn it.

That habitat is the reason this rig is built the way it is. Because a redfish is usually hooked close to shell and grass, every component here is an abrasion and control decision before it is a casting decision: an oyster-proof leader, braid that casts in wind and telegraphs soft bites, and a rod with enough backbone to turn a fish away from the nearest shell bed. Stealth, not power, is the theme — the tailing red you can see is the one you should catch.

The complete redfish rig at a glance

Rod7'0" medium-to-medium-heavy, fast action spinning rod — a good all-round inshore stick.
Reel2500–4000 spinning reel, sealed drag recommended (saltwater), 15–20 lb of drag.
Main line15–30 lb braid. Braid casts better in wind and gives you feel for soft bites over grass.
Leader2–4 ft of 20–40 lb fluorocarbon (or 30 lb mono) to resist oyster abrasion.
Hooks & weights1/0–4/0 circle hooks for live/cut bait; 1/4–1/2 oz jigheads for soft plastics; weedless hooks for sight-fishing in grass.
Top rigsPaddletail on a jighead, popping cork + shrimp, Carolina rig with cut bait, topwater, sight-fishing weedless plastic.

Rod and reel: the all-round inshore stick

A 7'0" medium-to-medium-heavy, fast-action spinning rod is the backbone of an inshore quiver because it does everything a redfish day demands: it loads up for accurate casts at a tailing fish, has the tip to work a paddletail or topwater, and carries enough backbone to turn a 30-inch fish away from the grass. Match it with a 2500–4000 spinning reel carrying 15–20 lb of drag. In saltwater, spend the extra money on a sealed drag — sand and salt eat unprotected reels after a season of wading and poling flats.

Line and leader: braid for feel, fluoro for shell

Run 15–30 lb braid as your main line. Braid casts better in the wind that always seems to blow on an open flat, and its lack of stretch lets you feel the soft, pick-up-and-drop bite of a redfish mouthing a bait over grass. The leader is where redfish rigs are won or lost: a 2–4 ft section of 20–40 lb fluorocarbon — or 30 lb monofilament if you want a cheaper, stiffer alternative — is your buffer against oyster and barnacle abrasion. Fluorocarbon also sinks and stays less visible in the clear, shallow water where reds tail. Retie whenever the leader shows wear; on an oyster flat, a nicked leader fails exactly when you need it.

The best redfish rigs, explained

Redfish will eat almost anything presented naturally, so the trick is choosing the rig that matches the water you are fishing. These five cover every common inshore situation.

Paddletail on a Jighead

The #1 artificial redfish bait, and the first thing to tie on when you are covering flats looking for active fish. Rig a 3–5 in swimbait on a 1/4–1/2 oz weighted hook and crawl or twitch it across the bottom, matching its pace to the current and the mood of the fish. Use it whenever you want to fish fast enough to find reds without spooking them.

Popping Cork + Shrimp / Live Bait

The classic marsh pattern and the best choice when water is stained or you need noise to call fish out of the grass. Pop the cork to draw attention, then let the shrimp or live bait drop into the disturbance and sit. Fish it along marsh drains, potholes and the edges of oyster points where reds cruise the moving tide.

Carolina / Bottom Rig with Cut Bait

The rig for bigger fish in deeper water. Soak cut mullet or shrimp on a bottom rig over deeper channels and drop-offs, and you are fishing where larger reds and winter bulls hold when they leave the skinny water. Use a 1/0–4/0 circle hook so the bait stays pinned and the fish hooks itself.

Topwater

For the most explosive strike in inshore fishing. Walk a noisy topwater across shallow flats and potholes at dawn, when reds push bait into the corners of the marsh, and be ready for a blow-up that looks bigger than the fish. If the water is calm and bright, switch to something quieter — a spook-style plug only works when reds are looking up.

Sight-Fishing with a Weedless Plastic

The ultimate presentation when you can see the fish. When a red is tailing in shallow water, skip a weedless-rigged soft plastic ahead of it and work the bait slowly past its face. This is the rig for slick, sunny days on the flats — cast well beyond the fish, never over it, and let the retrieve do the talking.

Live bait vs. artificials for redfish

Live shrimp and finger mullet are the top natural baits on any inshore water, and they are hard to beat when reds are finicky or the water is dirty. For bigger fish holding in deeper channels, cut mullet, ladyfish and blue crab are proven offerings. On the artificial side, keep paddletails, casting spoons, topwaters, soft-plastic shrimp imitations and scented Gulp-style baits in natural brown and chartreuse. Artificials earn their keep on clear flats, where you can fish them fast enough to cover water and make a precise cast to a single visible fish.

When and where to fish this rig

Learn to read the flats and you will find redfish most days. They hug structure — grass edges, potholes, points and drains — so work the edges rather than the open middle of a flat, especially on a falling or rising tide that moves bait along those lines. When reds are tailing in 6–12 in of water, they are feeding and catchable, but they spook easily: approach quietly and cast well ahead. In spring and summer, early mornings and the top of the tide put fish shallow; after a cold front, drop the same rigs into deeper marsh drains where reds stack up in warmer water, and in winter look to the surf line where bull reds to 40 lb move in to feed.

Common redfish mistakes

  • Casting on top of a fish you can see. Always cast well ahead of a tailing red and let the bait come to it.
  • Fishing the open middle of a flat instead of its structure edges, points and drains — that is where redfish actually hold.
  • Letting a hooked redfish reach the grass or oysters. Apply steady side pressure to turn its head before the leader touches the shell.
  • Ignoring cold fronts and keeping to the skinny water. When the temperature crashes, reds drop into deeper marsh drains and channels.
  • Fishing a frayed leader over oyster bars. One pass over shell can end the fight — check and retie constantly.

Frequently asked questions

What rod and reel do you need for redfish?

A 7 foot medium to medium-heavy fast-action spinning rod is the all-round inshore choice. Pair it with a 2500 to 4000 spinning reel with 15 to 20 lb of drag, and choose a sealed drag when you fish saltwater so sand and salt stay out of the reel.

What leader do you need around oyster beds?

Fish a 2 to 4 ft leader of 20 to 40 lb fluorocarbon, or 30 lb monofilament as an alternative. Redfish hug oyster bars and grass, so the leader is your abrasion buffer against shell — check it after every fish and retie the moment it frays.

How do you catch a tailing redfish?

Sight-fish it. When a redfish is tailing in 6 to 12 in of water, cast a weedless-rigged soft plastic well ahead of the fish and work it quietly past its face. Move slowly, keep your silhouette low, and stay off the edge of the flat so your wake does not give you away.
