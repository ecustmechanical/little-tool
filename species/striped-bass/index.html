Striped Bass Rig: The Complete Setup Guide

Scientific: Morone saxatilis Water: Freshwater & saltwater (coastal) Typical: 18–36 in · 3–20 lb Trophy: 40 lb+

Striped bass are the rare gamefish that splits one rig sheet into two venues. Along the Atlantic coast they roam the surf, bays and rivers in wide-ranging schools, while landlocked lakes grow hard-fighting fish on shad and herring. What connects both worlds is behaviour: stripers are aggressive, open-water feeders that chase baitfish schools and pin them against points, rips and the surface — so the whole presentation is about being where the bait is when the bass arrive.

That feeding style sets this rig's design rules. A striper's first move is a long, powerful run through current, rocks or breaking surf, which is why heavy line and sturdy hooks are non-negotiable on every venue. What changes is the rod length and reel size between the beach and the boat: pick the right outfit from this sheet and one small kit covers surf soaking, bay casting and lake trolling.

The complete striped bass rig at a glance

Rod7'0"–8'0" medium-heavy spinning/baitcast for boats and landlocked lakes; 10–12 ft surf rod (or 8–9 ft conventional) for beach fishing.
Reel4000–6000 spinning (lakes/inshore); surf 6500+ or conventional 5500–6500 for casting distance.
Main line20–30 lb mono/fluoro for jigging and casting; 30–50 lb braid in the surf (long casts, abrasion around rocks).
Leader2–4 ft of 20–40 lb fluorocarbon for jigging; 40–60 lb for rock-hopping surf and live bait.
Hooks & weights4/0–8/0 circle hooks for bait (they self-set and lip-hook); bucktail jigs and soft plastics on 1/2–3 oz jigheads.
Top rigsLive bait, bucktail/metal jig, topwater, swim shad, surf high/low rig.

Rod and reel: one striper, two venues

For boats and landlocked lakes, the workhorse is a 7'0"–8'0" medium-heavy spinning or baitcast rod. That length is long enough to launch a swim shad a full cast ahead of a moving school, yet short enough to fight a fish around dock pilings and creek bends, and it doubles for trolling. Pair it with a 4000–6000 spinning reel whose drag is smooth enough to survive a striper's first 50-yard run.

Beach fishing is a different sport. You are fighting distance, wind and breaking surf, so the rod becomes a 10–12 ft surf rod (or an 8–9 ft conventional rod if you prefer a casting reel). A surf-size reel in the 6500+ spinning class, or a 5500–6500 conventional, holds the heavy line and gives you the casting mass to reach the troughs and bars where stripers patrol beyond the breakers.

Line and leader: heavy for the wash, clean for the jig

Around structure and in the surf, abrasion beats visibility. 30–50 lb braid is the surf main line because it casts far, holds up against rocks and jetty stones, and gives you the strength to steer a big fish away from barnacles. Over that braid, run a 40–60 lb leader when you are rock-hopping or fishing live bait — this is the link that actually touches the structure. In cleaner boat and lake water, drop to 20–30 lb mono or fluorocarbon for jigging and casting, and when you do fish braid on a lake, a 2–4 ft, 20–40 lb fluorocarbon leader restores invisibility without costing casting distance.

The best striped bass rigs, explained

Stripers move to the bait, so your rig list is really a list of ways to intercept a moving school. Rotate these five as the situation changes.

Live Bait (Herring, Shad or Menhaden)

The big-fish pattern in both the ocean and freshwater, and the right call when bass are holding deep or turning down lures. Rig a whole herring, shad or menhaden on a 4/0–8/0 circle hook — freelined where the water is clear, or below a fish-finder sinker when current is pushing. With a circle hook, point the rod at the fish, let it run, then reel tight; the hook sets itself in the corner of the mouth.

Bucktail Jig / Metal Jig

The #1 artificial pattern for current and deep structure, and the go-to once a surface blitz sounds and the fish drop. Use it around bridges, rips, ledges and deep creek channels, working a rhythmic lift-drop that keeps the jig in contact with the bottom. Size the head from 1/2 oz to 3 oz so it stays down in the tide you are fishing.

Topwater (Pencil / Spook)

For dawn and dusk, when stripers push bait to the surface and the water explodes. A pencil popper or walk-the-dog plug twitched fast over breaking fish draws strikes you can hear before you feel. Have one tied on whenever birds and bait are working the top — blitzes last only minutes.

Swim Shad / Soft Plastic

The best way to match the hatch on any venue. Pick a swim shad or soft-plastic paddletail in the size and color of the local baitfish — bunker, herring or shad — rig it on a weighted jighead, and cast to the edges of a blitz or troll it through a holding zone. It covers water efficiently when bass are scattered.

Surf Rig (High/Low)

The classic beach setup for soaking bait beyond the breakers. A pyramid sinker grips the sand in the wash while high and low droppers keep your cut bait just above the bottom. Use it when you are fishing from shore and want to present bait to stripers holding in the outer troughs and bars.

Live bait vs. artificials for stripers

Freshness is everything with striper bait. Menhaden (bunker), herring, shad, eels and mackerel — cut or whole — are the proven baits, and a lively offering out-fishes a tired one every time. On the artificial side, carry bucktails, casting metals, pencil poppers, walk-the-dog plugs, swim shads and soft-plastic paddletails in baitfish patterns. Since stripers key on schools of bait rather than on structure alone, the forage you imitate matters more than the color: size and silhouette first, flash second.

When and where to fish this rig

Stripers reveal themselves at predictable moments. At dawn and dusk they push baitfish to the surface — that is your topwater window — and after dark they roam into the shallows and along flats hunting the same schools. In rivers and inlets, fish the outbound tide, because moving water stacks bait on points and channel edges where stripers lie in ambush. On landlocked lakes in summer, look for shad schools on your electronics and work the edges of the bait, keeping a topwater tied on for early light and a metal jig ready for the moment the fish sound. Spring brings herring and shad runs into river systems, and fall pushes coastal fish into a feeding frenzy that makes every rig on this sheet productive.

Common striped bass mistakes

  • Casting into the middle of a blitz instead of its edges — baitfish escape at the perimeter, and that is where stripers actually feed.
  • Swinging a hard hook-set on circle hooks. Point the rod at the fish, let it run, then reel tight.
  • Fishing river inlets on the wrong tide. Learn the local tide table and save your casts for the outbound push that stacks bait on the points.
  • Reaching the beach with a boat rod. Surf fishing needs a 10–12 ft rod and heavy line simply to get past the breakers.
  • Not having a topwater tied on at first light, when the surface erupts and you only have minutes to make the cast.

Frequently asked questions

What rod do you need for striped bass?

For boats and landlocked lakes, a 7 to 8 foot medium-heavy spinning or baitcast rod covers casting and trolling. From the beach, step up to a 10 to 12 foot surf rod, or an 8 to 9 foot conventional rod, so you can cast past the breakers and keep line tight over the wash.

What size circle hook should you use for striped bass?

Use 4/0 to 8/0 circle hooks for bait. Circle hooks self-set and hook the corner of the mouth, so with live or cut bait you point the rod at the fish, let it run, then reel tight — never swing a hard hook-set.

What line and leader do you need for surf fishing stripers?

Surf fishing calls for 30 to 50 lb braid for long casts and abrasion around rocks, paired with a 40 to 60 lb leader when you are rock-hopping or fishing live bait. When jigging in cleaner water, 20 to 30 lb mono or fluorocarbon does the job, or add a 2 to 4 ft leader of 20 to 40 lb fluorocarbon over braid to keep the presentation subtle.
